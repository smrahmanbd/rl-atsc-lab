RL-based traffic signal control: what has been built and what is still open

A plain-language tour for someone who has finished the Foundation lesson. It ends with eight research ideas, each with a first experiment you could run in SUMO.

Study guide (PDF)Slides (PPTX)Slides (PDF)

1 · Where RL sits

Signal control has developed in steps. Each step uses more live information and fixes less in advance. RL is the first step that learns its own decision rule, and in research it is compared against the steps below it.

Fixed-timeA clock replays a plan designed offline.
ActuatedDetectors extend or skip a green.
AdaptiveSCATS and SCOOT adjust split, cycle and offset to measured flow.
Max-pressureServe the phase with the largest queue difference.
Reinforcement learningLearns its own rule from reward.

2 · Pressure, the idea that links traffic theory and RL

In Foundation the reward was minus the cars waiting. That reward will push cars into a road that is already full. Pressure fixes this: for one movement it is the queue coming in minus the queue on the road it feeds. A phase's pressure is the sum over its movements. Max-pressure control serves the phase with the highest value, and PressLight uses pressure as the RL reward.

3 · Landmarks

You do not need every paper. Know what each landmark added, because new work is usually described as an earlier method plus one change.

2013
Max-pressure (Varaiya). Serve the phase with the largest queue difference. No learning, and a proof that queues stay bounded.
2018
IntelliLight. Deep RL for one junction, tested with real traffic data.
2019
FRAP, PressLight, CoLight, MA2C. Phase competition, pressure as the reward, and agents that cooperate.
2020
MPLight runs one shared network at 2,510 simulated Manhattan signals. AttendLight handles junctions of different shapes with one model.
2021
RESCO and LemgoRL. Realistic benchmarks; earlier methods turn out to be brittle.
2023
SafeLight, UGAT, missing-data methods. Attention moves to safety, sim-to-real and faults.
2025
LLMLight uses a language model as the controller. Traffic-R1 reports a six-week field A/B test at 10 intersections.
2026
Sim2Signal benchmarks the sim-to-real gap. LATS and DGLight combine language models with RL.

4 · Many junctions

One agent cannot control a city. With four phases per junction, ten junctions have 410 = 1,048,576 combined actions. Research therefore gives each junction its own agent (multi-agent RL). The agents usually share one network, and they coordinate through the reward or by exchanging messages. The hard part is that every agent's neighbours are learning at the same time, so the traffic each one sees keeps shifting.

5 · Testing fairly

Almost every result comes from a simulator, usually SUMO or CityFlow, and from shared benchmarks such as RESCO, LibSignal and LemgoRL. Before trusting a claim, check four things:

Strong baselines

Tuned fixed-time, actuated control and max-pressure. Beating an untuned timer shows little.

Several seeds

RL training is noisy. Look for a mean and a spread, and compare the gain with the spread.

Unseen traffic

Test on demand the agent did not train on, and with faults such as a failed detector.

Who is counted

Vehicles only, or people, including bus passengers and pedestrians?

6 · Seven gaps between papers and streets

RL controllers look strong in papers and are rare on streets. Open each gap for the evidence and the directions people are trying.

1. Sim-to-real

Simulated drivers, detectors and signals differ from real ones, so a controller trained in a simulator meets a different world.

Evidence. Sim2Signal (2026) tested 33 kinds of mismatch. Moving a controller across unchanged made it worse every time, and no single fix won everywhere.

Directions. Grounded action transformation (UGAT, 2023); language-model-informed grounding (PromptGAT, 2024); realistic benchmarks with real signal logic (LemgoRL, 2021).

2. Robustness to new traffic

Demand on the street differs from the demand used in training.

Evidence. Kwesiga et al. (2026) report 11–32% less delay than optimised actuated control, but a model trained on one demand pattern degraded when demand changed a lot. RESCO (2021) found earlier methods brittle to changed sensing assumptions.

Directions. Train on varied demand; stress-test on unseen patterns before claiming a gain.

3. Safety

A learning agent can propose a change that is unsafe or not allowed.

Evidence. SafeLight (2023) builds road-safety standards into learning and reports significantly fewer collisions. LemgoRL places a regulatory signal-logic unit around the agent.

Directions. A rule layer outside the agent; action masking so illegal phases cannot be chosen.

4. Missing data

Detectors fail, and most studies assume perfect sensing.

Evidence. Mei et al. (2023) impute missing states and rewards so RL controllers keep working at junctions without sensors. LA-Light (2024) reports a 20.4% waiting-time gain over RL controllers under sensor outage.

Directions. Imputation; controllers that accept gaps; a language model as a fallback reviewer.

5. Explainability

Operators and regulators need to see why a phase was chosen.

Evidence. Ault et al. (2020) learned a policy simple enough to inspect and report up to 19.4% less delay than actuated control. LLMLight's written rationales were rated by 15 human experts.

Directions. Simple policy forms; written explanations, with the open question of whether they match the real reason.

6. Fairness

Maximising vehicle throughput can leave pedestrians, bus passengers and minor roads waiting.

Evidence. Zhang et al. (2026) reward the number of delayed people, including pedestrians and transit riders, on a Melbourne corridor.

Directions. People-based rewards; hard maximum-wait limits; multi-objective RL.

7. Deployment

There is rarely a route from a paper to a controller cabinet.

Evidence. Max-pressure reached hardware-in-the-loop testing on a commercial controller in Minnesota (2024). Traffic-R1 (2025) reports a six-week A/B test at 10 intersections.

Directions. Hardware-in-the-loop, shadow-mode trials, staged roll-out, and evidence a road authority can audit.

7 · Eight research ideas

Each idea starts from a gap above and can be begun with one junction or a short corridor. Starter ideas fit a course project, Intermediate ideas fit an MSc thesis, and the Advanced idea needs more computing and reading.

Starter

1. Does it still work on traffic it never saw?

Robustness is the most common reason a result fails to transfer.

First experiment. Train one agent on a single demand pattern and another on a mix. Test both on three unseen patterns.

Measure. Delay on unseen patterns, and the drop from seen to unseen.

Start reading: Kwesiga et al. 2026; RESCO 2021

Starter

2. Which reward is best at my junction?

Queue, waiting time and pressure can lead to different behaviour.

First experiment. Train the same agent three times, changing only the reward.

Measure. Delay, longest queue, and the longest wait any vehicle had.

Start reading: PressLight 2019; Efficient pressure 2021

Starter

3. What does a safety shield cost?

A deployable controller must obey minimum green and maximum wait.

First experiment. Add a rule layer that overrides illegal actions. Train with it and without it.

Measure. Delay with and without the shield, and how often it overrides.

Start reading: SafeLight 2023; LemgoRL 2021

Intermediate

4. What happens when a detector fails?

Real detectors drop out.

First experiment. At test time, blank one approach's queue reading for 10%, 30% and 50% of the time.

Measure. Delay against failure rate, for RL and for actuated control.

Start reading: Mei et al. 2023; LA-Light 2024

Intermediate

5. People or vehicles?

A bus with 40 passengers counts as one vehicle in most rewards.

First experiment. Add buses and a pedestrian phase. Compare a vehicle-based reward with a people-based one.

Measure. Person-delay, pedestrian maximum wait, and car delay given up.

Start reading: Zhang et al. 2026; Levin 2023

Intermediate

6. How big is the sim-to-real gap for one parameter?

It shows which simulator assumption matters most.

First experiment. Train with default driver settings. Test with slower reactions and gentler acceleration.

Measure. Delay change per parameter; rank the parameters.

Start reading: UGAT 2023; Sim2Signal 2026

Intermediate

7. Can the learned policy be written as rules?

Operators approve what they can read.

First experiment. Train a DQN, then fit a small table or decision tree to its choices.

Measure. How often the rules agree with the DQN, and the delay lost.

Start reading: Ault et al. 2020

Advanced

8. Can a language model help the RL controller?

Language models carry general knowledge but are slow and can be wrong.

First experiment. Let a language model review only unusual states, such as an emergency vehicle, and compare with RL alone.

Measure. Delay in normal and rare cases, response time, invalid outputs.

Start reading: iLLM-TSC 2024; LATS 2026; DGLight 2026

A recipe that works for any of them

  1. Write the question as one sentence with a measurable outcome.
  2. Fix the scenario: network, demand, simulator settings, decision interval.
  3. Set the baselines first: tuned fixed-time, actuated, and max-pressure.
  4. Change one thing at a time.
  5. Run at least five seeds and report the mean and the spread.
  6. Test on traffic the agent did not train on.
  7. Report what did not work.

References

Title, authors, year and venue of each entry were checked against a live source page in October–November 2026. Where a page names no venue, the entry says arXiv preprint, even if the paper has since been published elsewhere. Results are quoted only where the paper states them.

Foundations

Christopher J. C. H. Watkins, Peter Dayan (1992). Q-learning. Machine Learning 8, 279-292.
Introduces and proves convergence of the Q-learning update used in the Foundation level.

Volodymyr Mnih and 18 co-authors (2015). Human-level control through deep reinforcement learning. Nature 518, 529-533.
The Deep Q-Network: a neural network in place of the Q-table, made stable with replay memory and a target network.

Richard S. Sutton, Andrew G. Barto (2018). Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd edition). MIT Press.
The standard textbook: agents, rewards, value functions, temporal-difference learning, Q-learning and function approximation.

Max-pressure control

Pravin Varaiya (2013). Max pressure control of a network of signalized intersections. Transportation Research Part C: Emerging Technologies (2013).
Introduces max-pressure control for signalized networks: a decentralized policy using only local queue information with a proven maximum-throughput (queue-stability) property.

Michael W. Levin (2023). Max-Pressure Traffic Signal Timing: A Summary of Methodological and Experimental Results. Journal of Transportation Engineering, Part A: Systems, 149(4).
State-of-the-art review of max-pressure variants (cycle-based, realistic flow models, measurement error, multimodal access) and their simulation and early field evidence.

Raphael Stern, Michael W. Levin, Amir Kiani (2024). Toward Implementation of Max-Pressure Control on Minnesota Roads: Phase 2. Minnesota Department of Transportation, Report MN 2024-26.
Hardware-in-the-loop testbed running max-pressure on a Q-Free MaxTime controller, showing MP can be safely deployed on North American signal hardware.

RL methods

Hua Wei, Guanjie Zheng, Huaxiu Yao, Zhenhui Li (2018). IntelliLight: A Reinforcement Learning Approach for Intelligent Traffic Light Control. Proc. 24th ACM SIGKDD Int. Conf. on Knowledge Discovery & Data Mining (KDD 2018).
An early deep RL signal controller tested with real camera-derived traffic data, with attention to interpreting the learned policy.

Tianshu Chu, Jie Wang, Lara Codeca, Zhaojian Li (2019). Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning for Large-scale Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:1903.04527.
A decentralised multi-agent advantage actor-critic (MA2C) for adaptive signal control, with two techniques that let each local agent see more of its surroundings and learn more easily.

Hua Wei, Nan Xu, Huichu Zhang, Guanjie Zheng, Xinshi Zang, Chacha Chen, Weinan Zhang, Yanmin Zhu, Kai Xu, Zhenhui Li (2019). CoLight: Learning Network-level Cooperation for Traffic Signal Control. Proc. 28th ACM Int. Conf. on Information and Knowledge Management (CIKM 2019); arXiv:1905.05717.
Uses graph attention so neighbouring signal agents communicate and coordinate, reported to outperform state-of-the-art methods on a network with hundreds of signals.

Hua Wei, Chacha Chen, Guanjie Zheng, Kan Wu, Vikash Gayah, Kai Xu, Zhenhui Li (2019). PressLight: Learning Max Pressure Control to Coordinate Traffic Signals in Arterial Network. Proc. 25th ACM SIGKDD Int. Conf. on Knowledge Discovery & Data Mining (KDD 2019), pp. 1290-1298.
Grounds the RL reward in max-pressure theory (linked to throughput maximisation) with a compact state, outperforming conventional and prior learning-based controllers on arterials.

Guanjie Zheng, Yuanhao Xiong, Xinshi Zang, Jie Feng, Hua Wei, Huichu Zhang, Yong Li, Kai Xu, Zhenhui Li (2019). Learning Phase Competition for Traffic Signal Control. Proc. 28th ACM Int. Conf. on Information and Knowledge Management (CIKM 2019), pp. 1963-1972.
FRAP models competition between phases by demand, giving invariance to flips/rotations, faster convergence and better generalisation across intersection structures.

Chacha Chen, Hua Wei, Nan Xu, Guanjie Zheng, Ming Yang, Yuanhao Xiong, Kai Xu, Zhenhui Li (2020). Toward A Thousand Lights: Decentralized Deep Reinforcement Learning for Large-Scale Traffic Signal Control. Proc. AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, 34(04), 3414-3421.
MPLight: decentralized deep RL using pressure-based regional coordination and a reward that lets agents coordinate implicitly, demonstrated on a real-world Manhattan network of 2,510 traffic lights.

Afshin Oroojlooy, Mohammadreza Nazari, Davood Hajinezhad, Jorge Silva (2020). AttendLight: Universal Attention-Based Reinforcement Learning Model for Traffic Signal Control. Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 33 (NeurIPS 2020).
A single attention-based policy that handles intersections with different numbers of roads, lanes and phases without retraining, outperforming classical and RL baselines in single- and multi-environment regimes.

Qiang Wu, Liang Zhang, Jun Shen, Linyuan Lü, Bo Du, Jianqing Wu (2021). Efficient Pressure: Improving efficiency for signalized intersections. arXiv preprint arXiv:2112.02336.
Introduces efficient pressure (EP) and builds Efficient-MP and the Efficient-XLight RL family, evaluated on multiple real-world datasets.

Simulators and benchmarks

Pablo Alvarez Lopez, Michael Behrisch, Laura Bieker-Walz, Jakob Erdmann, Yun-Pang Flötteröd, Robert Hilbrich, Leonhard Lücken, Johannes Rummel, Peter Wagner, Evamarie Wießner (2018). Microscopic Traffic Simulation using SUMO. 21st IEEE Int. Conf. on Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITSC 2018), pp. 2575-2582.
Reference description of the open-source SUMO microscopic simulator widely used for TSC, V2X and CAV studies.

Lucas N. Alegre (2019). SUMO-RL. GitHub repository (software).
Gymnasium/PettingZoo-compatible RL environments for traffic signal control on top of SUMO.

Huichu Zhang, Siyuan Feng, Chang Liu, Yaoyao Ding, Yichen Zhu, Zihan Zhou, Weinan Zhang, Yong Yu, Haiming Jin, Zhenhui Li (2019). CityFlow: A Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning Environment for Large Scale City Traffic Scenario. The World Wide Web Conference (WWW 2019); arXiv:1905.05217.
A fast, scalable microscopic simulator for city-scale multi-agent RL, the default platform for most PressLight/CoLight/LLMLight-family studies.

James Ault, Guni Sharon (2021). Reinforcement Learning Benchmarks for Traffic Signal Control. Proc. NeurIPS Track on Datasets and Benchmarks, Vol. 1 (2021).
RESCO toolkit and realistic SUMO benchmark scenarios; finds earlier methods are brittle to changed sensing assumptions and irregular intersections.

Arthur Müller, Vishal Rangras, Georg Schnittker, Michael Waldmann, Maxim Friesen, Tobias Ferfers, Lukas Schreckenberg, Florian Hufen, Jürgen Jasperneite, Marco Wiering (2021). Towards Real-World Deployment of Reinforcement Learning for Traffic Signal Control. 20th IEEE Int. Conf. on Machine Learning and Applications (ICMLA 2021), DOI 10.1109/ICMLA52953.2021.00085; arXiv:2103.16223.
LemgoRL: a realistic benchmark of the German town of Lemgo with a signal-logic unit enforcing regulatory and safety constraints, aimed at deployable RL.

Hao Mei, Xiaoliang Lei, Longchao Da, Bin Shi, Hua Wei (2023). LibSignal: An Open Library for Traffic Signal Control. Machine Learning (Springer), DOI 10.1007/s10994-023-06412-y; arXiv:2211.10649.
Open library benchmarking RL-TSC models across both SUMO and CityFlow with calibrated environments and shared protocols.

Evidence on the gaps

James Ault, Josiah P. Hanna, Guni Sharon (2020). Learning an Interpretable Traffic Signal Control Policy. arXiv preprint arXiv:1912.11023.
Learns regulatable polynomial (interpretable) signal policies via modified Deep Q-learning, reducing delay up to 19.4% vs. actuated control while remaining inspectable.

Longchao Da, Hao Mei, Romir Sharma, Hua Wei (2023). Uncertainty-aware Grounded Action Transformation towards Sim-to-Real Transfer for Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:2307.12388.
UGAT applies grounded action transformation only when its uncertainty is low, narrowing the transition-dynamics gap between simulator and target environment for RL-TSC.

Wenlu Du, Junyi Ye, Jingyi Gu, Jing Li, Hua Wei, Guiling Wang (2023). SafeLight: A Reinforcement Learning Method toward Collision-free Traffic Signal Control. Proc. AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence, 37(12), 14801-14810.
Safety-enhanced residual RL with multi-objective loss and reward shaping based on road-safety standards, significantly reducing collisions while improving mobility.

Hao Mei, Junxian Li, Bin Shi, Hua Wei (2023). Reinforcement Learning Approaches for Traffic Signal Control under Missing Data. Proc. 32nd Int. Joint Conf. on Artificial Intelligence (IJCAI 2023), DOI 10.24963/ijcai.2023/251.
Imputes missing states (and rewards) so RL controllers keep working at intersections without sensors, staying robust across missing-data rates.

Dickens Kwesiga, Angshuman Guin, Khaled Abdelghany, Michael Hunter (2026). Evaluating the Robustness of Reinforcement Learning based Adaptive Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:2603.15283.
RL controller with field-realistic eight-phase ring-barrier structure cuts delay 11-32% vs. optimised actuated control but degrades under substantially different O-D demand unless trained on diverse patterns.

Ferdous Al Rafi, Susrik Mukherjee, Latika Liladhar Dekate, Jennifer Yawa Lavoe, Huaiyuan Yao, Shlok Mohanty, Longchao Da, Xuesong Zhou, Hua Wei (2026). Sim2Signal: Sim-to-Real Benchmarks for Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:2609.01676.
Decomposes the sim-to-real gap into observation, action, transition and reward gaps and tests 18 mitigation methods over 33 gap settings and 10 calibrated networks; direct transfer always degrades and no mitigation wins everywhere.

Xiaocai Zhang, Neema Nassir, Lok Sang Chan, Milad Haghani (2026). Human-Centric Traffic Signal Control for Equity: A Multi-Agent Action Branching Deep Reinforcement Learning Approach. arXiv preprint arXiv:2602.02959.
MA2B-DDQN rewards minimising the number of delayed people (pedestrians, occupants, transit riders) on a Melbourne corridor, shifting TSC objectives from vehicles to travellers.

Language models with RL

Longchao Da, Minquan Gao, Hao Mei, Hua Wei (2024). Prompt to Transfer: Sim-to-Real Transfer for Traffic Signal Control with Prompt Learning. Proc. AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI 2024); arXiv:2308.14284.
PromptGAT uses LLM cloze-style prompts to infer how weather, road type and traffic state alter system dynamics, then grounds a DQN agent's actions to those dynamics, narrowing the sim-to-real performance gap.

Aoyu Pang, Maonan Wang, Man-On Pun, Chung Shue Chen, Xi Xiong (2024). iLLM-TSC: Integration reinforcement learning and large language model for traffic signal control policy improvement. arXiv preprint arXiv:2407.06025.
LLM-assisted RL: an RL agent proposes the phase and an LLM checks and corrects unreasonable decisions (degraded communication, rare events such as emergency vehicles), reducing average waiting time by 17.5% vs. RL alone under degraded communication.

Maonan Wang, Aoyu Pang, Yuheng Kan, Man-On Pun, Chung Shue Chen, Bo Huang (2024). LLM-Assisted Light: Leveraging Large Language Model Capabilities for Human-Mimetic Traffic Signal Control in Complex Urban Environments. arXiv preprint arXiv:2403.08337.
LA-Light places an LLM at the centre of control and gives it perception and decision tools (static/dynamic traffic queries, existing TSC algorithms); in the authors' simulation platform it adapts without retraining and cuts average waiting time by 20.4% vs. RL controllers under sensor-outage conditions.

Siqi Lai, Zhao Xu, Weijia Zhang, Hao Liu, Hui Xiong (2025). LLMLight: Large Language Models as Traffic Signal Control Agents. Proc. ACM SIGKDD Conference on Knowledge Discovery and Data Mining (KDD 2025); arXiv:2312.16044.
Uses an LLM as the signal-control agent, prompted with real-time traffic state and reasoning step by step, plus LightGPT, a backbone fine-tuned for TSC; on ten real and synthetic datasets it is reported to beat nine traditional/RL baselines and ten general LLMs, with interpretability rated by fifteen human experts.

Xingchen Zou, Yuhao Yang, Zheng Chen, Xixuan Hao, Yiqi Chen, Chao Huang, Yuxuan Liang (2025). Traffic-R1: Reinforced LLMs Bring Human-Like Reasoning to Traffic Signal Control Systems. arXiv preprint arXiv:2508.02344.
A 3-billion-parameter language model trained with offline then online RL for signal control; reports a six-week A/B test at 10 intersections in a Chinese city with a reduction in average queue length of roughly 9% against manual control.

Chenbo Yu (2026). DGLight: DQN-Guided GRPO Fine-Tuning of Large Language Models for Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:2604.25259.
Uses a frozen CoLight-based Deep Q-Network as a critic that scores a language model's candidate actions, and trains the language model on those scores.

Zirui Yuan, Siqi Lai, Hao Liu (2026). CoLLMLight: Cooperative Large Language Model Agents for Network-Wide Traffic Signal Control. International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR 2026); arXiv:2503.11739.
Network-level cooperative LLM agents using a spatiotemporal graph of neighbouring intersections, complexity-aware reasoning depth and simulation-feedback fine-tuning of a lightweight LLM; reported to outperform state-of-the-art methods on synthetic and real datasets.

Yifeng Zhang, Peizhuo Li, Tingguang Zhou, Mingfeng Fan, Guillaume Sartoretti (2026). LATS: Large Language Model Assisted Teacher-Student Framework for Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning in Traffic Signal Control. arXiv preprint arXiv:2603.24361.
A language model acts as teacher, producing features that describe each intersection; a small student network learns to reproduce them, so the final RL controller runs without the language model.

Surveys

Hua Wei, Guanjie Zheng, Vikash Gayah, Zhenhui Li (2019). A Survey on Traffic Signal Control Methods. arXiv preprint arXiv:1904.08117.
Reviews conventional transportation approaches to signal control alongside reinforcement learning studies, to encourage work across the two fields.

Kaiwen Zhang, Zhiyong Cui, Wanjing Ma (2024). A survey on reinforcement learning-based control for signalized intersections with connected automated vehicles. Transport Reviews, 44(6), 1187-1208.
Systematic review of RL for CV/CAV-data signal control, RL trajectory planning for CAVs and cooperative signal-CAV control, with a research roadmap.
